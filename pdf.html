Blighted : Urban Renewal in Post-WWII Los Angeles

Contributors: Caroline Sanchez

This publication is an investigation of post-WWII (1945-1960s) urban renewal programs in Los Angeles and their disproportionate impact on minority communities. To facilitate economic growth, construction of the new city was shaped along racial and economic lines. Urban renewal programs deliberately shaped Los Angeles’ spatial configuration into what we know it as today.

A Changing Landscape: Impact of WWII on American Cities

Rise of the Automobile
By 1930, the majority of Americans enjoyed mass produced cars by the dominant automobile company: the Ford Motor Company.1 Automobiles had become as common as household appliances and one in every five people owned one; in Los Angeles, car ownership was as high as one in every three people. 2 It offered increased transportation ease, allowing previous city dwellers to abandon their overcrowded homes in the city and move to cheaper suburbs at a growing rate.3

Suburbanization / City Decentralization
Suburban neighborhoods were made up of quiet, attractive single-family homes that were increasing in demand and hundreds of new subdivisions opened annually. In 1920, 346 subdivisions were recorded, a number that jumped to 1,434 three years later in 1923.4 The Palos Verdes Estates, for example, had amenities and other recreational benefits attractive for both business and ordinary people to relocate to.5 Suburbs presented an opportunity for the middle class to abandon racially heterogeneous, rapidly declining cities and establish a more secure physical presence further away. The automobile allowed them to commute to city centers where their places of employment remained. By the 1960s, almost every urban area was divided into a poor, central downtown, and a ring of suburban communities on the outskirts of it.6 Because of this development, city funds moved away from public transportation to automobiles and roadways instead.

Deindustrialization
Beginning in the 1950s, manufacturing cities across the Midwest that had produced specialized products faced economic decline.7 Factories began to leave cities as advancements in automation allowed them to shrink their workforces. Companies that had built American cities and directly contributed to their wealth concentration began to leave for the suburban frontier and out of the U.S. in search of cheaper labor markets.8 The decoupling of industry and cities was facilitated by the completion of the highway system in the 1960s and automobile/trucking industries. This economic process had a devastating impact on American cities, many of which lost a vibrant public life and the economic wealth/investment that heavy manufacturing industries –steel, tires, automobiles– had offered.9

Emergence of “Blighted” Neighborhoods

With people, business, and wealth moving out of downtown city centers, what and who was left behind? Black, minority, and working-class communities. The substandard housing they occupied was quickly redlined –a discriminatory practice in which banking institutions denied neighborhoods services and basic financial investment. The ability of homeowners to leave neighborhoods that were being “blighted” by a Black population and obtain homeownership elsewhere was made possible by long-standing local and federal practices to contain African American homeownership within specific neighborhoods.

The Los Angeles HOLC and City Surveys
The Home Owners’ Loan Corporation (HOLC) worked concurrently with the Federal Housing Authority (FHA), an agency that guaranteed federally backed mortgages, to encourage banks to increase their lending practices.1 To optimize these federally backed mortgages, the federal government created a set of standards for assessing the level of risk. This assessment resulted in the HOLC City Surveys, a rating of over 200 Los Angeles neighborhoods to determine the best mortgage lending practices.2 The surveys created four categories of standardized property appraisals to be used by banking institutions: A (green), B (blue), C (yellow), and D (red). Green neighborhoods like Beverly Hills were new, racially homogenous, and occupied by business and professional men.3 Blue neighborhoods were second grade, but still desirable; yellow neighborhoods were declining; and red neighborhoods were old, majority Black, and occupied by the working class. The main factors noted in these surveys were: population, percentage of foreign families, nationalities, negroes, and shifting or infiltration. Specifically, neighborhoods were analyzed based on the influx of non-whites, a pattern described as “subversive racial elements increasing”.4

The preservation of “A” neighborhoods was made possible through various methods. Real estate agents and white homeowners would use legal and illegal methods, from lobbying for zoning changes to engaging in property destruction, to try and keep the neighborhood white.5 In Southern California, zoning deeds created by homeowners’ associations were used to explicitly prevent Blacks and Asians from buying property in affluent white neighborhoods.6 The FHA explicitly promoted white homogeneity and racial segregation in its official handbook, Planning Profitable Neighborhoods7 by recommending “the use of racially restrictive covenants to prevent ‘undesirable’ mixing of the races” in new suburbs.8

By dividing residential neighborhoods into four distinct categories, the HOLC prohibited federal investment into poor, minority neighborhoods and influencing private businesses to do the same. This redlining resulted in neighborhoods like Boyle Heights and Watts falling into disrepair and decline, quickly turning into slums whose residents were unable to escape due to housing discrimination. Once slums, they’d be slated for slum clearance projects. In other words, the language used by federal institutions to categorize various neighborhoods perpetuated racial division and destruction of communities of color under the name of maintaining property values and home ownership standards.

Highway Construction in the 1950s

As cities, including Los Angeles, attempted to create a new, massive highway infrastructure, federal and state measures worked together to fund construction. The 1947 Collier-Burns Highway Act established a highway tax fund to serve as the revenue for freeway construction across the state.1 Supplemented by the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, which initiated the construction of a national network of highways spanning 41,000 miles and linking cities from coast to coast, freeway construction in L.A. was massively boosted for the next three decades.2 When deciding where highways, interchanges, and entrances/exists were going to be built, city planners had to determine a criteria or best practice for location screening. Engineers, city planners, and professionals used graphs, flow charts, and research to justify construction decisions to ignore the political implications of their decisions.3 But highways were most often built atop and through “blighted” neighborhoods identified from the HOLC City Survey files.

The 1958 Master Plan was supposed to ensure that every Los Angeles resident was within 4.5 miles of a freeway; however, only 61% of this framework was completed.4 Freeways meant to be constructed in white neighborhoods –Beverly Hills Freeway, Pacific Coast Freeway, and Laurel Canyon freeway—never happened.5 In the 1950s, State Route 2 was originally designed to run atop Santa Monica Boulevard, connecting La Cañada Flintridge to the Santa Monica Pier, and cutting through Beverly Hills.6 The largely white, middle-class population mobilized, using connections, resources, and news outlets to stage an effective political protest against the proposed freeway.7 This revolt was successful and today, the freeway is located seven miles east in Echo Park. Echo Park had also staged protests against highway construction but unlike Beverly Hills, they lacked a significant number of resources and political clout to have any effect. 8 But the best example of racialized freeway construction can be found in one of the nation’s largest Spanish-speaking neighborhoods: Boyle Heights. The HOLC classified the racially diverse neighborhood red. Of the planned freeways in the 1958 Master Plan, 100% of East Los Angeles’ initially planned freeways were built –six freeways and two interchanges less than one mile apart.9

The East Los Angeles interchange is made up of twenty-three lanes, thirty-two bridges, and covers 135 acers.10 It was the pinnacle of post-WWII public works projects, becoming the first freeway in the country to surround any downtown area.11 Meant to allative heavy traffic congestion, it has instead produced one of the most traffic-congested transit systems in the country. Furthermore, the interchange has resulted in one of the densest pockets of air pollution, contributing to respiratory issues, cancer, and other health diseases.12 Saint Isabella, a Catholic church and school attended by many Boyle Heights residents, and Hollenbeck Park, home to a man-made lake where picnics and community events were held, were destroyed.13 Despite Whittier Boulevard acting as a commercial corridor not unlike Sunset Blvd –home to markets, theaters, restaurants, and more—it was chosen for dissection by city planners.14

Downtown Revitalization

Highways had worked as a conduit for people, jobs, homes, and wealth out of cities and into suburbs, and slum clearance. To accommodate the decentralized city growth spurred by suburbanization, city planners prioritized efficiency and the “public good,” almost always at the expense of cutting up racially diverse, working-class neighborhoods. These “public goods” included cultural institutions.

Bunker Hill
Located downtown near the financial district, Bunker Hill was a prime-spot neighborhood characterized by its diverse residents and lack of proper infrastructure. According to a 1940 census, of the 6,978 housing units, over 4,000 lacked toilets/private baths, over 1,000 lacked running water, and over 600 needed major repairs.1 Once a wealthy enclave in the late 1800s, Bunker Hill had lavish Victorian mansions lining Grand Avenue.2 Thirty years later, many of these mansions were retrofitted into boarding houses to accommodate the neighborhood’s residents.3 Eager to tear down the eyesore of weathered mansions, new plans to raze the neighborhood entered development. In March 1959, the Bunker Hill Urban Renewal Project was adopted by the Community Redevelopment Agency (CRA), and 135 acres were tentatively chosen as the site for new development.4 Around six thousand low-income residents were originally slated to be removed, but the project ended up displacing around ten thousand Angelenos without compensation.5

The apartment buildings were never built, and the land was developed for private business instead. An engineering report issued in May 1956 determined that a conversion of the residential property to hotels, a civic trade plaza, auditorium, office buildings, and high-end apartments would produce maximum profit.6 Local elites and some city officials used their influence to centralize the city’s high culture and create a music hall despite public hearings and submittals of alternative plans. The Dorothy Chandler Music Pavilion opened its doors in December of 1964, the first of many buildings –some of which include the Mark Taper Forum (1967) and the Ahmanson Theatre (1967).7 The elite-oriented structures were meant to make downtown Los Angeles more attractive and elevate it to the social level of other city’s. Construction of the Walt Disney Concert Hall began in 1999, ten years after its first conception and thirty years after the last Victorian houses are moved.8

The Chavez Ravine / Dodger Stadium

Inner-city residential displacement by freeway construction was a trend continued by other projects. Originally slated to become a federal housing project providing 3,360 housing units, the Chavez Ravine instead became Walter O’Malley’s Dodger Stadium, home to Los Angeles’ beloved baseball team.

The Chavez Ravine contained three communities: Palo Verde, La Loma, and Bishop.1 Despite the lack of proper infrastructure, from running water to electricity and pavement, the Chavez Ravine was home to a vibrant Mexican-American community. In 1950, the L.A. Housing Authority selected it to become the site of a $10 million dollar housing project sponsored by the federal government. The residents who vacated, some after being coerced and physically removed, were promised, in writing, first choice of the new housing that was going to be built.2

As the L.A. Housing Authority planned their public housing project, Los Angeles mayoral candidate Republican Norris Poulson campaigned on downtown revitalization. After winning, Mayor Poulson immediately cancelled public housing projects despite ongoing contracts and construction.3 The Chavez Ravine, already cleared, was ideally positioned to be utilized as the city saw fit: a vehicle to revitalize their downtown districts via sports.4 Luckily, New York Dodgers’ owner Walter O’Malley wanted to move out of Brooklyn. The outdated Ebbets Field where the Dodgers played was in an increasingly congested and racially integrated neighborhood.5 In 1953, Mayor Poulson changed the wording of the Chavez Ravine deed to allow the transfer of public property to private business and helped O’Malley secure a contract to subsize the construction of Dodger Stadium, a privately-owned stadium built using federal funds.6

Poulson’s actions were made possible by simultaneous rise of redbaiting. The Cold War had effectively turned public opinion on any political candidate or policy about public housing into socialist and communist plots.7 After a very narrow referendum on a ballot to approve the deal in 1958, the Dodgers and Dodger stadium were approved to move to Los Angeles.8 With no modern, functional, and public housing units to speak of, the residents of Chavez Ravine scattered across Los Angeles wherever they could.

Conclusion

The prosperity enjoyed by select members of society –most often the white middle class who found homeownership and economic opportunity through federal assistance as well as private business development– was limited in scope.1 Once residents in idyllic, racially homogenous neighborhoods, these select Americans were physically removed from the old downtown neighborhoods and the minorities that occupied them. As U.S. cities continued to be starved of public life and economic investment, their residents and their struggles steadily worsened. Rather than reinvesting in downtown neighborhoods, city planners turned towards urban renewal, an undertaking that often resulted in the destruction the old city structure and its residents for the “greater good.”

Today, Los Angeles neighborhoods are still shaped by patterns of development and gentrification. UCLA’s Urban Displacement Project has tracked neighborhood transformations across Los Angeles, Orange, and San Diego. Between 1990 and 2015, gentrified tracts in L.A. County increased by 16%.2 In Little Tokyo, one of the last Japan towns in the country, closures, evictions, and relocations threaten decade-long businesses.3 The rising rents have forced businesses, many decades old, to leave. Luckily, it has inspired resistance from residents. When Suehiro Cafe was forcibly relocated, a street protest calling attention to the issue was planned and now, the cafe is coming back several blocks away from its original location.4 Beyond local efforts, the National Trust for Historic Preservation has helped preserve homes, neighborhoods, and landmarks vulnerable to redevelopment. Their publishing of Philadelphia’s Chinatown as a historic preservation has helped Little Tokyo put in their own application.5 But Little Tokyo is just one neighborhood. Inglewood, Highland Park, and Boyle Heights face pressure from developers and residents face rising housing costs if not outright displacement. It is not a new trend.
