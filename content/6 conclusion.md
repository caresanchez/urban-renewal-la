The prosperity enjoyed by select members of society –most often the white middle class who found homeownership and economic opportunity through federal assistance as well as private business development– was limited in scope.[^1] Once residents in idyllic, racially homogenous neighborhoods, these select Americans were physically removed from the old downtown neighborhoods and the minorities that occupied them. As U.S. cities continued to be starved of public life and economic investment, their residents and their struggles steadily worsened. Rather than reinvesting in downtown neighborhoods, city planners turned towards urban renewal, an undertaking that often resulted in the destruction the old city structure and its residents for the “greater good.” 

Today, Los Angeles neighborhoods are still shaped by patterns of development and gentrification. UCLA’s Urban Displacement Project has tracked neighborhood transformations across Los Angeles, Orange, and San Diego. Between 1990 and 2015, gentrified tracts in L.A. County increased by 16%.[^2] In Little Tokyo, one of the last Japan towns in the country, closures, evictions, and relocations threaten decade-long businesses.[^3] The rising rents have forced businesses, many decades old, to leave. Luckily, it has inspired resistance from residents. When Suehiro Cafe was forcibly relocated, a street protest calling attention to the issue was planned and now, the cafe is coming back several blocks away from its original location.[^4] Beyond local efforts, the National Trust for Historic Preservation has helped preserve homes, neighborhoods, and landmarks vulnerable to redevelopment. Their publishing of Philadelphia’s Chinatown as a historic preservation has helped Little Tokyo put in their own application.[^5] But Little Tokyo is just one neighborhood. Inglewood, Highland Park, and Boyle Heights face pressure from developers and residents face rising housing costs if not outright displacement. It is not a new trend.
