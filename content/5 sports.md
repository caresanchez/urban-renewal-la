Inner-city residential displacement by freeway construction was a trend continued by other projects. Originally slated to become a federal housing project providing 3,360 housing units, the Chavez Ravine instead became Walter O’Malley’s Dodger Stadium, home to Los Angeles’ beloved baseball team. 

The Chavez Ravine contained three communities: Palo Verde, La Loma, and Bishop.[^1] Despite the lack of proper infrastructure, from running water to electricity and pavement, the Chavez Ravine was home to a vibrant Mexican-American community. In 1950, the L.A. Housing Authority selected it to become the site of a $10 million dollar housing project sponsored by the federal government. The residents who vacated, some after being coerced and physically removed, were promised, in writing, first choice of the new housing that was going to be built.[^2] 

{% figuregroup '2' 'cr-view-2, cr1' %}

As the L.A. Housing Authority planned their public housing project, Los Angeles mayoral candidate Republican Norris Poulson campaigned on downtown revitalization. After winning, Mayor Poulson immediately cancelled public housing projects despite ongoing contracts and construction.[^3] The Chavez Ravine, already cleared, was ideally positioned to be utilized as the city saw fit: a vehicle to revitalize their downtown districts via sports.[^4] Luckily, New York Dodgers’ owner Walter O’Malley wanted to move out of Brooklyn. The outdated Ebbets Field where the Dodgers played was in an increasingly congested and racially integrated neighborhood.[^5] In 1953, Mayor Poulson changed the wording of the Chavez Ravine deed to allow the transfer of public property to private business and helped O’Malley secure a contract to subsize the construction of Dodger Stadium, a privately-owned stadium built using federal funds.[^6] 

Poulson’s actions were made possible by simultaneous rise of redbaiting. The Cold War had effectively turned public opinion on any political candidate or policy about public housing into socialist and communist plots.[^7] After a very narrow referendum on a ballot to approve the deal in 1958, the Dodgers and Dodger stadium were approved to move to Los Angeles.[^8] With no modern, functional, and public housing units to speak of, the residents of Chavez Ravine scattered across Los Angeles wherever they could.
